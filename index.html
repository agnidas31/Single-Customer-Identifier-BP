BP · Single Customer Identifier · Customer data

From Loyalty Members to Every Customer

Every purchase on a bp forecourt left three separate records behind: a till receipt, a card payment and, sometimes, a loyalty swipe. None of them shared a key, so the business could count baskets, cards or members, but never customers. The Single Customer Identifier linked all three into one view, and said how confident it was in every link.

The project

One view of every forecourt customer

The goal was simple to say and hard to do: take three transaction systems that were never designed to talk to each other, and join them into one record per customer, across the UK, Germany and Poland. I brought in the raw data, built the common data model, and wrote the matching models that make the links.

The starting point: one purchase, three records

A single visit could show up in the till system (what was bought), the payment system (how it was paid) and the loyalty system (who bought it, but only if they swiped). Each system had its own IDs, its own clocks and its own idea of a transaction.

Loyalty only ever covered a minority of spend. Everyone who paid by card but never swiped a loyalty card was, in effect, invisible.

The method: narrow, score, link

There was no list of known matches to train against, so this was an unsupervised problem. I narrowed the search first: a till record could only match a payment from the same site, date, country and basket type. Within that small window, I scored each candidate pair on how closely their time, value and quantity agreed, and kept the best one.

Exact keys did most of the work, but the scored matches added roughly a fifth more coverage on every pair. That's the slice a simple join would have thrown away.

How sure is each match?

At a busy site, two or three baskets of a similar value can land in the same minute. So every link carries a confidence score: the similarity of the pair, scaled down by how many other candidates were nearly as close. Only links above a threshold are kept.

Most real matches score very high, so coverage barely moves until the threshold passes 0.7, then falls away fast. That knee is where we set it: strict enough to trust, loose enough to keep almost everything worth keeping.

Who we could suddenly see

Once the links were in place, linked transactions rolled up into one record per customer, including the people loyalty data had never captured.

The largest group by far was customers who paid by card but never swiped loyalty. They had been shopping with bp all along; the business just couldn't see them.

What I handed over

The data foundationRaw EPOS, Payment and Loyalty data brought in from each source system, cleaned into one common model per market, with the in-scope population defined for every pair so “unmatchable” never counted as “missed”.
The matching modelsBlocking keys, similarity scoring, dampening and a 0.7 threshold, tuned per market, producing a customer ID that downstream reporting and models could build on.

Why this mattered

A customer key, built once and used everywhere

Matching three datasets was never the point. The point was being able to ask customer questions at all: who shops with us, who crosses from the pump to the store, who is drifting away. The cross-shopping insight behind the 38% conversion lift came straight from this view, and the Customer Lifetime Value model that followed was built on top of it.
